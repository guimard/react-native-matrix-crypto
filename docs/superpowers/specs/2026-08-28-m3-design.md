# react-native-matrix-crypto M3 design: device verification by SAS, and sender authenticity

## 0. Authority, scope, and what this document deliberately leaves open

The M1 design, `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-27-react-native-matrix-crypto-design.md`,
remains the binding authority for everything it settles: naming, crate layout, the
core/FFI boundary, crypto agility, the no-logger rule, federation neutrality, and the
public facade's shape. The M2 design,
`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-28-m2-encryption-core-design.md`, is binding for
everything it settled inside M2's scope. This document extends both, for M3 only.

Where this document and either predecessor disagree inside M3's scope, this one wins and
says why. Everywhere else they win.

### 0.1 What M3 contains, and why it is four things rather than nineteen

This document was first written as a record of everything M2 leaves behind: nineteen
backlog items and nine unanswered design questions. That record was accurate and is kept
in full. What it was not is a milestone. It described the whole remaining surface, and the
milestone sequence that follows from it is longer than what the work in front of it needs.

What changed is the situation, not the evidence. The library ships `0.1.0` first, and M3
lands as a later version while a React Native application is being built against `0.1.0`.
M3's contents were chosen against one test: **verification, plus whatever would obstruct a
consumer building on top of `0.1.0`.** Not the full milestone the record described.

M3 contains four things.

1. **Device verification by short authentication string comparison.** SAS, not QR. That
   settles §7 Q1. QR is deferred rather than rejected, and Q1 keeps what it would have
   cost: a dependency absent from `rust/Cargo.lock`, an off-by-default Cargo feature, and
   a size budget that has already tripped once. See §5.1, V.
2. **`verification_state` reaching the consumer**, which is what turns a successful
   decryption into a claim about who sent it. **That it ships is settled. What shape it
   takes on the public surface is not**, and stays §7 Q3 with its options intact. See
   §5.1, B1.
3. **A typed `SyncDelta`**, so a product stops hand-writing a five-field rename against an
   `unknown` parameter with no compile-time help. The shape already exists in three places
   in this repository and every consumer writes a fourth. See §5.1, B3.
4. **The signal delivery latency**, measured in seconds in a release build, with the
   spawn-one-operating-system-thread-per-signal design named as the suspect and
   `spawn_blocking` on the runtime the core already owns named as the candidate. See
   §5.1, B2.

Everything else is in §5.2. It is recorded, not deleted, and §5.2 says why in its own
words: the reasoning behind an item is worth more than the item, and rediscovering it
costs more than keeping it.

**The distinction to hold on to while reading.** §5.1 is M3's work. §5.2 is known,
evidenced, and unowned. §10 is something else again, and older: the product boundaries M1
drew, which no milestone moves. Three different kinds of "not now", and only the first of
them is a schedule.

### 0.2 What this document still refuses to be

It is not a design of device verification. The scope decision above settles what M3
contains, and it settles the method for one of the four. It does not settle how any of it
is shaped. Every remaining design choice sits in §7 with its options and their costs, and
each carries a status saying whether the scope decision **settled** it, **narrowed** it,
took it **out of M3** without deciding it, or left it **untouched**.

That status line is the most important thing this document does. A guess written as
settled is indistinguishable from an inherited ruling six weeks later, and the next reader
has no way to tell them apart. So §7 answers exactly the questions the scope decision
actually answered, says so in each case, and answers no others. Of the nine: one is
settled, one is out of M3 without being decided, two have one half settled and one half
open, and **five are live choices M3 still has to make.** Those five are what a reader
should expect to be asked about.

It is also not an implementation plan. A plan follows a spec the user has approved.

**One constraint specific to this file.** It is tracked and the repository is public.
Hostnames, account names, credentials and paths into the operator's own infrastructure
stay in the ledger, which is not published. Here they are "the development homeserver",
"the operator's production homeserver" and "the operator's deployment repository".

## 1. What M2 leaves true, and how each of it was proven

### 1.0 The standard of evidence, which is itself inherited

M2 converged on one rule and applied it to implementations, to reviews and to gates
alike: **a check nobody has watched fail is not known to work.** Reading a check tells
you what it intends, never what it does.

In practice that meant mutation. Sever the wiring and confirm that exactly the expected
test goes red; plant the violation and confirm the gate rejects it; break the property in
two different ways and confirm two tests fail for two different reasons rather than one
test having a spare. The reviews that found the most were the ones that reproduced a
claim rather than agreeing with its reasoning, and one of them priced a deferral by
writing the deferred test, discovering the estimate was wrong by about two orders of
magnitude in the direction that justified not doing the work (G33).

Everything in §1 is stated with the mutation that makes it falsifiable, because a
property asserted without one is exactly what this milestone spent its time finding.

### 1.1 Properties proven inside this codebase, against ourselves

| Property a consumer can rely on | Where it lives | What made it falsifiable |
|---|---|---|
| One crypto machine per process, held by the library, never a handle returned to JavaScript | `machine.rs` | Reopen test pointed at a different store directory and watched to fail |
| Creating twice with a matching config resolves against the existing machine; a mismatched config rejects with `already_initialised` | `machine.rs` | Concurrency regression tests; `init` is serialised, so the race the brief assumed exists cannot occur at all |
| A store surviving process restart, encrypted with the passphrase the product supplies | `machine.rs`, `matrix-sdk-sqlite` with `crypto-store` and `bundled` | Passphrase wiring severed, exactly two tests went red |
| The core owns one multi-threaded tokio runtime and enters it around machine work | `runtime.rs`, `machine.rs::with_machine` | `in_runtime` stripped from `with_machine`: exactly the three tests that reach a live machine panic with "there is no reactor running", the three that do not keep passing |
| Two parties in one process exchange a group key and each decrypts the other's event, with the key travelling through the request pump | `tests/two_parties.rs` | The test's `with_machine` back door was deleted and it still passes; ten of fourteen argument transpositions reproduced by the reviewer, all caught |
| The pump's ordering is keys-query, then keys-claim, then sendToDevice | `session.rs::share_scope_key` | Claim wiring disabled: the share delivers `m.room_key.withheld`; restored, it delivers `m.room.encrypted` |
| `shareScopeKey` tracks the users it is given | `session.rs` | Deleting the tracking call fails both the unit test and the two-party keys-query assertion |
| No argument transposition survives the FFI delegate | `matrix-crypto-ffi/tests/delegate_order.rs` | Fourteen transpositions applied one at a time and reverted |
| No error-variant transposition survives the core-to-FFI mirrors | `matrix-crypto-ffi/tests/error_mapping.rs` | Both planted swaps caught; the collapse mutation is caught by this file alone, which is what shows it does not overlap the other two |
| Errors carry no payload content | `session.rs` | Structural, not disciplinary: `SessionError`'s variants are fieldless with fixed literals, the payload struct derives no `Debug`, and decrypted vectors are only ever measured with `.len()` |
| Signals never block the emitting thread and are never emitted under the machine lock | `observer.rs` | Two non-redundant tests: making `emit` a no-op fails delivery only; making it synchronous and inline fails only the deadlock test, at 60.01 seconds |

Two of those rows deserve their reasoning rather than only their result.

**The runtime row is where M2 nearly repeated M1a's defining mistake.** `build` was
wrapped in `in_runtime`; the accessor was not. Every `OlmMachine` method the encryption
work needed would have panicked on the shipped path, and all sixteen tests passed anyway,
because `#[tokio::test]` supplies an ambient runtime the shipped path does not have. That
is the same shape as M1a's nineteen green tests against an assumed UniFFI error shape: the
suite was proving the harness rather than the code. The fix round's requirement was
therefore not "wrap the accessor" but "add a test that is not a `#[tokio::test]` and
drives a genuinely async machine method through `with_machine`". M3 inherits that
requirement for any new async surface it adds.

**The tracking row was invisible until a test's own convenience gave it away.** The
two-party test reached past the public surface to make the counterparty's devices tracked,
and both the implementer and the reviewer initially read that as test convenience. It was
the gap announcing itself: upstream's `mark_tracked_users_as_changed` skips users the
machine has never seen, a sync payload's `changed_devices` routes only there, and
`with_machine` is not re-exported by the FFI crate, so no JavaScript consumer could have
done what the test did. A product would have called `shareScopeKey`, received no error,
and encrypted to nobody. The proof demanded of the fix was therefore "delete the test's
back door and it must still pass" (G34).

### 1.2 Properties proven against a third-party implementation

This distinction is load-bearing, the README draws it in public, and M3 must keep drawing
it. Two of our own machines agreeing proves the implementation is self consistent; it
cannot prove the wire format is right, because a consistent misreading of the protocol
passes it cleanly on both sides.

Two independent proofs exist, both against `matrix-nio` 0.26, a Matrix client written in
Python by people who have never seen this code, and both in both directions:

* **At the core.** `rust/matrix-crypto-core/tests/level_two_interop.rs`, marked
  `#[ignore]` so an ordinary `cargo test` needs no network. `scripts/run-level-two-interop.sh`
  runs it, standing up a throwaway Continuwuity homeserver in a container pinned by
  digest, creating the account it needs, installing a pinned `matrix-nio[e2e]` into a
  temporary virtualenv, and destroying all of it. CI runs the same script as
  `interop-level-two`, in about two and a quarter minutes on a standard hosted runner:
  no self-hosted runner, no KVM, no privileged mode.
* **Through the published TypeScript surface.** `packages/example-app/level-two/run_level_two.py`
  drives the same exchange on an Android emulator through the facade, the generated
  TypeScript, the JSI binding and the UniFFI scaffolding, reporting `LEVEL2_SUMMARY 13/13`
  across three consecutive clean runs. Importing the root entry is what installs the JSI
  host object, verified by the reviewer, so JSI is genuinely in the path rather than
  nominally.

**What this does not prove, stated as plainly as the README states it.** `matrix-nio` 0.26
and this library both call `vodozemac 0.10.0`, so the proof has a floor and the floor is
the ratchet. A defect inside that crate, or a misreading shared below the protocol line,
passes both sides. What two independent implementations genuinely test is everything above
it: event shapes, the `/keys/*` payloads a real homeserver accepts and answers, to-device
routing, and the order a session key has to travel in. That is where this library's own
code lives.

**The control that nearly lied, because M3 will be offered the same trap.** The first
green run of the core-level proof was worthless and the implementer proved it worthless
itself (G42). The corruption control flipped a base64 character of a copy of an
already-sent event, and it failed as expected. A mutation run showed it also failed with
the bytes left intact: a copy of a delivered event carries an already-seen megolm message
index, and nio rejects a repeated index as a replay whatever the ciphertext says. The
control was testing replay detection, not cryptography, and would have passed against a
library that could not encrypt at all. The generalisation, which is worth more than the
fix: **corrupting a copy of a delivered artifact tests replay detection rather than the
property you meant.** Both level 2 proofs now corrupt freshly encrypted events, and the
facade-level one avoided the trap by having read about it.

The second proof also earned its cost twice over, in a way that settles an argument about
whether it was worth building. It feeds a real seven-field `/sync` body verbatim and
asserts `malformed_payload`; a step written from the facade's own documentation as it read
at the time would have asserted the opposite and failed on first launch. One of its
mutations is that old documentation, executed.

### 1.3 Properties proven on real hardware

`PROBE_SUMMARY 12/12` on an Android emulator and on an iOS simulator, exercising a record
round trip, a byte array, an async call, a typed error, one callback signal, real identity
keys, and a full encrypt and decrypt round trip recovering 52 plaintext bytes byte for
byte through the public facade only. Proven failable: one corrupted base64 character gives
`round_trip FAIL rejected with kind "undecryptable"` and `10/12`, with the denominator
holding at twelve. A reviewer went further and made the suite constructor itself reject,
so seven checks never existed, and got `PROBE_SUMMARY 5/13`: the denominator grew, every
owned check reported FAIL, and only the constructor name leaked rather than the thrown
message. The summary cannot lie about how many checks it was supposed to run.

`probe-android` runs this in CI, against a release APK, asserting the verbatim
`PROBE_SUMMARY 12/12` line host-side rather than a zero exit. **It found a real defect on
its first run**, which is the whole argument for it: the Rust-to-JavaScript observer
callback arrives after the promise in release builds, and debug had always won the race,
so every prior manual run had passed (G48). See §5, B2, for what that defect became.

### 1.4 Properties proven about the shipped artifact

* **Size.** The packed tarball is 67555 KB, 44% of the roughly 150 MB gate M1 set, down
  74.4% from the 263523 KB the same source produces as a `staticlib`. Recorded in
  `artifact-sizes.json` as `task11-cdylib-post-t2`. Verified through the example app's own
  Gradle build, the real autolinking path a consumer takes, rather than a bare
  `cargo build`. The staticlib baseline is 263523 KB rather than the 184608 KB measured
  before M2, so the change absorbed M2's own dependency growth rather than only closing a
  pre-existing gap.
* **SQLite is statically bundled on Android**, proven from the artifact rather than the
  configuration: `llvm-readelf -d` on the built `.so` finds only `libdl`, `libm` and
  `libc` in `NEEDED`, with no `libsqlite3.so`.
* **A consumer needs no Rust toolchain**, proven by the `cold-consume` job installing the
  packed tarball with `cargo` and `rustc` unreachable on `PATH`.
* **Seven gates exist and six run in CI.** `gate:workspaces`, `gate:boundary`,
  `gate:drift`, `gate:stubs`, `gate:logger`, `gate:agility` run in the `gates` job;
  `gate:readme` is defined in the root `package.json` and appears in no workflow step.
  See §5, B11.
* **The no-logger claim is now true as published**, because it was narrowed rather than
  defended (G49). See §2.7.

## 2. What M2 leaves false or absent

### 2.1 A decrypted event does not prove who sent it. This is M3's reason to exist

`EventEnvelope.sender` is the sender field the homeserver delivered on the outer,
not-yet-decrypted event. Decryption does not verify it. `algorithm` is likewise read from
the incoming event rather than asserted by this library. For all of M2 both are
**unauthenticated transport metadata**, and both the specification (§7.1), the public
README and the type's own doc comment say so in those terms.

This is recorded here with its history because the history is the part that generalises.
During implementation an instruction was issued to either surface the authenticated sender
from `encryption_info` or change the documentation to stop implying one existed. **The
first option does not exist**, and the implementer refused it with the upstream source as
evidence: `EncryptionInfo::sender`
(`matrix-sdk-common-0.18.0/src/deserialized_responses.rs:334`) is documented upstream as
untrusted data unless the `verification_state` is `Verified` as well, and `sender_device`
carries the same caveat. There is no stronger sender value sitting in `encryption_info`
waiting to be exposed. The authentication comes from device verification (G22).

Two things survive from that exchange:

* The instruction was right to offer an escape hatch rather than only a demand. An
  instruction with no exit produces either a wrong change or a stall.
* The consequence is larger than the fix. It is a property of what ships, not a
  code-comment detail, which is why it became spec §7.1 and why `verification_state`
  reaching the public surface is listed as M3 work rather than as an implementation note.

A product that reads the sender of a successfully decrypted event as the cryptographic
sender has assumed something the library does not provide, and that assumption is the shape
impersonation takes.

### 2.2 Nothing is verified, so both halves of the trust decision are the permissive ones

**Inbound**, decryption uses `TrustRequirement::Untrusted`. Anything stricter rejects every
event, because nothing is verified.

**Outbound**, `share_room_key` takes `EncryptionSettings::default()`, which carries
`CollectStrategy::AllDevices`, which upstream marks as not recommended per the guidance of
MSC4153 because it shares with every unblacklisted device rather than only devices signed
by their owner.

Both are forced by the same absence, and both construction sites now carry the same
comment, `// M2: verification lands in M3; revisit this with it.`, so one grep finds the
whole decision (`session.rs:63` and `session.rs:729`).

**The asymmetry is the part worth carrying into M3.** The inbound choice had to be written,
so it was argued for and documented. The outbound one arrived free with `default()` and
would have shipped without anyone deciding it; it was found only while verifying something
else (G38). The general shape: **a choice that must be written gets argued, and a choice
that arrives as a default gets inherited.** In a cryptographic library those are the same
kind of decision and deserve the same scrutiny. M3 adds a verification state machine with
settings of its own, and every one of those settings has a default.

### 2.3 No identity is published, so there is nothing to verify against

The recommended identity-based collect strategy gives room keys to nobody whose identity
is unpublished, and nothing in the shipped surface publishes a cross-signing identity.
This is why §2.2's outbound choice is currently forced rather than chosen, and it is why
moving off `AllDevices` is a question about ordering rather than a switch to flip. See §7,
Q4.

### 2.4 Six facade functions still throw `not_implemented`

`restoreCryptoMachine`, `getDeviceStatuses`, `requestVerification`, `confirmVerification`,
`exportSecrets`, `importSecrets` (`facade.ts:132,479,483,487,491,495`). They exist as final
types that compile and reject at runtime with a typed error, which is the M1a arrangement
that lets a consuming team build against the real shape.

Their frozen signatures constrain M3 and are reproduced here because §7's questions turn on
them:

```ts
getDeviceStatuses(userId: string): Promise<DeviceStatus[]>   // DeviceStatus = { deviceId, trust }
requestVerification(userId: string, deviceId: string): Promise<string>
confirmVerification(verificationId: string, data: unknown): Promise<void>
exportSecrets(passphrase: string): Promise<Uint8Array>
importSecrets(bundle: Uint8Array, passphrase: string): Promise<void>
```

`TrustState` is `'unverified' | 'recognized' | 'verified'`, and unlike `CryptoAlgorithm`
and `CryptoErrorKind` it is a **closed** union. That is a decision M3 will have to either
keep or break deliberately, not discover.

### 2.5 Two error kinds fold a distinction M3 is expected to split

`UnknownDevice` covers two different conditions and its own doc comment says so: an
identity that does not match what the machine has on record, which nothing the user does
can fix, and a device that does not meet the trust level the call requires, which
verifying the device fixes. **The second is unreachable in M2** precisely because §2.2's
inbound requirement is the most permissive one. The moment M3 makes the trust requirement
configurable, the second becomes reachable, and a product cannot tell "verify this device"
from "this can never work".

`UnsharedSession` folds the withheld reasons the same way. See §5, B7 and B8.

**Why this matters more than an ordinary taxonomy complaint.** G20 recorded the sharper
version of it: two defects concealed each other, because with a coarse kind both the
pre-cryptography path and the real MAC-failure path produced the same value, so the test
could not tell them apart. A finer taxonomy would have failed the test and exposed both.
**A coarse taxonomy does not merely lose information for the product, it disables the tests
that would have caught the coarsening.**

### 2.6 The layer with the least automated coverage is the layer where the one documentation defect lived

The core-level level 2 proof runs in CI on every pull request. The facade-level one does
not: `.github/workflows/ci.yml` contains six jobs, and none of them runs
`packages/example-app/level-two/run_level_two.py`. It needs an emulator leg that also has
Docker, and that leg does not exist.

That gap is not hypothetical. The facade's own published documentation told a consumer to hand
`receiveSyncChanges` the whole `/sync` response verbatim, while the guard eleven lines
above rejected exactly that: a real `/sync` body's eight top-level keys have zero overlap
with the five recognised names. The published instruction described a call that always
fails, and it was found by a review reading code, not by any test, because no test looked
there (G45). The guard was ruled correct and kept; the sentence was replaced with a worked
mapping example.

### 2.7 The no-logger promise is narrower than it was, and the narrowing is the honest part

M1's published claim was that the bridge has no logger, and the gate said to enforce it had
never opened a C++ file. `cpp/generated/matrix_crypto.cpp` writes to `std::cout` at five
sites, all on `catch (const jsi::JSError &error)` paths in the UniFFI callback plumbing,
and that file is committed, shipped in the tarball, and compiled into what a consumer
links. On iOS it ships as source through the podspec and compiles into the consumer's own
app.

What was established rather than assumed (G44, G49): each site writes a fixed literal plus
`jsi::JSError::what()`, which is a JavaScript exception's message and stack; at every one
the function installed is the generator's own, so no argument, signal, ciphertext, key or
identifier is interpolated; a stack trace still discloses function names and source
locations; `strings` on the release AAR finds four of the five literals plus an imported
`std::cout` in every ABI, at `-O2`, with no `NDEBUG` guard; and the generator cannot be
configured to omit them, on four independent negatives.

**The claim was narrowed rather than kept.** `gate:logger` now scans every shipped C, C++
and Objective-C source and tolerates exactly that three-line shape, and only inside a path
`gate:drift` regenerates. The composition is the argument: the shape bounds what may be
written, and drift's empty diff proves the file really is the tool's output rather than a
hand-planted imitation. Six plants were each watched failing, including the composition
itself. A promise that is true is worth more than one that is absolute and wrong, and M3
inherits both the narrowed promise and the reason it is narrow.

## 3. The two failure shapes this project keeps producing, and which M3 will be offered again

M2's most useful specification sections were the ones that recorded a mistake and its
correction rather than only the conclusion. Both of the shapes below cost this milestone
real time, more than once each, and both are still available to M3.

### 3.1 One true sentence standing in for a different, false one

Twice, a statement that was true was silently read as a different statement that was false,
and both readings passed every in-process test.

* "The bridge does no networking" became "the bridge produces nothing to send". The
  consequence was a library that would encrypt to nobody, hold keys nobody could find, and
  never learn that any of it happened. Corrected as spec §3bis, which added
  `takeOutgoingRequests` and `markRequestSent`.
* "The machine knows about the device" became "the machine can reach it". A group key
  travels wrapped in an Olm session, and no Olm session exists until this device claims a
  one-time key. Every share to a real device was emitting `m.room_key.withheld` with code
  `m.no_olm`, a message saying the key could not be sent. Corrected as spec §3ter, which
  named the ordering: keys-query, then keys-claim, then sendToDevice.

Both were found one layer deeper than the layer that introduced them, and both were
specification gaps before they were code defects.

A third instance had a caller-visible consequence neither party had stated until it was
forced out: **the first share to a never-seen user delivers nothing, by construction.** It
tracks the user and arms a keys query that only reaches the homeserver through the next
`takeOutgoingRequests`, so no device is known yet. The product must pump, mark, and share
again. The library cannot hide this, because it performs no request and therefore cannot
wait for one (G36). That is now written out in the public README as a worked sequence
rather than a sentence, because the rule only makes sense in the shape of a real flow.

M3's version of this hazard is worth naming in advance without pretending to know its
answer: "this device is verified" and "this event was sent by a verified device" are two
different statements, and so are "the sender's identity is verified" and "the sending
device is signed by that identity". Upstream keeps them apart in `VerificationLevel`, whose
variants are `UnverifiedIdentity`, `VerificationViolation`, `UnsignedDevice`,
`None(DeviceLinkProblem)` and `MismatchedSender`. Which of those distinctions reach a
consumer is §7, Q3.

### 3.2 A check that reports success without having examined its target

The ledger numbers ten of these, and the count is not a joke about carelessness. The eighth
appeared in work built specifically to close a verification gap, written by an agent that
had read about the previous seven; the ninth and tenth appeared in the work built to close
the eighth, in the same file, one of them surviving a review that had just watched its
neighbour being fixed.

1. An agility regex defeated by camelCase, so `MegolmSession` passed while `megolm_session`
   failed (M1).
2. A typecheck whose `include` never reached the `interop/` directory.
3. A no-logger gate passing because its targets had moved and it scanned nothing.
4. A corruption test flipping a leading character, so the event failed to deserialise
   before any cryptography ran: it proved malformed JSON is rejected, nothing more.
5. `cargo fmt --all --check` run from the repository root, where there is no root
   `Cargo.toml`, so it prints usage and exits 0. Every task in the milestone ran it that
   way, because the dispatch template said so.
6. The boundary gate refusing to pass when the crate is missing, but not when the extracted
   dependency list is empty. Settled by feeding the extraction synthetic empty input and
   watching it pass, after two wrong answers reached by reading the script.
7. `cargo test` exiting 0 having matched no test at all, printing
   `0 passed; 0 failed; 1 filtered out`. Caught before it could bite, by someone who had
   read about the other six.
8. A summary runner asserting `passed == total` without pinning what `total` should be, so
   dropping a step prints a clean pass on a smaller denominator (G54).
9. `scripts/measure-signal-latency.sh`'s check that the two arms of a B2 measurement report
   different emission builds, which could not fire in any run: the function it read from
   wrote its row to stdout *and* returned the build id there, so the caller compared a
   string beginning with the arm's own label, which is forced to differ. Three tracked files
   asserted the behaviour. Found by a reviewer making it face the case it rejects.
10. The same file's diagnostic for a launch that printed no `PROBE_EMIT_BUILD` line, and the
    `NONE` fallbacks beside it: `grep` matching nothing exits 1, `pipefail` promotes it and
    `set -e` aborts the assignment, so the run died at the right exit code with nothing
    said. Written a hundred lines below a comment explaining that exact mechanism and fixing
    it for a neighbouring call. `shellcheck` passes on both 9 and 10.

Three more of the same family are recorded separately: nothing ran the on-device probe
automatically, so a regression introduced early in M2 sat undetected for the whole
milestone (G39); the example app's typecheck was run by nothing, and trunk was broken by it
(G43); and the no-logger gate did not scan the file where the violation actually lived
(G44).

The generalisation the milestone paid for repeatedly: **reading a check tells you what it
intends, never what it does.** And G54's sharper corollary, which is the one to carry into
M3: it is what happens by default whenever **the thing reporting and the thing being
reported are the same thing**. A denominator declared by the artifact under test is not a
denominator.

A further instance was found while writing this document and is listed as an inherited item
rather than folded into the count above: see §5, B11.

**What 9 and 10 changed, beyond the count.** Both were found by running the case, both
survived `shellcheck` and both survived reading. The tenth survived a review cycle in which
the ninth was found and fixed twelve lines away, because the manual pass that demonstrated
the fix covered the cases someone had thought of, and this was not one of them. That is not
a lapse of care; it is what manual verification is. `gate:measure-guards` now faces every
one of that file's refusals with the case it refuses, on every commit, and asserts both the
exit status and a distinctive substring of each diagnostic -- because instance 9 exits with
the wrong status and instance 10 exits with the right one and says nothing, so either
assertion alone lets one of them through.

## 4. Decisions M3 inherits and may not quietly reopen

Restated because M3's implementers will not read two predecessor specs end to end.

**From M1.** The bridge never talks to a homeserver. It has no logger, as narrowed by
§2.7. `sender` carries `@user:server` verbatim and no primitive distinguishes a local from
a federated participant. No public identifier may say `megolm`, `olm` or `room`, enforced
by an agility gate that splits identifiers on case transitions. `matrix-crypto-core` never
gains a direct `uniffi` dependency; all logic lives there and `matrix-crypto-ffi` mirrors,
converts and delegates. Every core-to-FFI conversion destructures its source, so a field
added later fails the build instead of being silently dropped. Generated code is never hand
edited. `panic = "unwind"` stays, because `uniffi_core` turns a Rust panic into a catchable
TypeScript error and that is the bridge's only panic safety net.

**From M2.** One machine per process, held by the library, no handle returned to
JavaScript. The pump ordering of §3ter. `takeOutgoingRequests` returns an unordered set,
and marking is not optional bookkeeping but the thing that advances the state machine.
`receiveSyncChanges` accepts the five-field subset documented at `facade.ts:205`, not a raw
`/sync` body, and rejects a non-empty object that names no recognised field at all, while
tolerating unknown fields a homeserver adds. `decryptEvent` takes the scope as its first
parameter. `storePassphrase` is required and explicitly nullable. Reviews do not reproduce
what requires credentials: a reviewer that labels a result "credible, corroborated, not
reproduced" is worth more than one that quietly implies it ran something it did not.

**The rule for breaking a frozen signature**, which M3 will need at least once and should
apply rather than re-derive. It is not "never break one", it is **"never break one
gratuitously"**, and the milestone applied that test three times with two different answers:

* G1 kept `device_identity_keys`'s two parameters, because dropping them bought nothing the
  alternative did not, and would have cascaded through generated bindings and a working
  on-device probe.
* G9 broke `CryptoMachineConfig` to add `storePassphrase: string | null`, because the frozen
  shape could not express a security property the spec mandates, and an optional field can
  be omitted by accident and yields unencrypted key material with no signal.
* G25 broke `decryptEvent` to take the scope, because the frozen shape could not express a
  required input, and the alternative smuggled a required argument inside an `unknown`
  where the type system could not see it, trading a compile error for a runtime error in a
  cryptographic API.

#### B2. Signal delivery latency, measured in seconds in a release build

*Status: in M3.* Kept whole because it already names its own suspect and its own
candidate, and §8 asks for a before-and-after measurement rather than an assertion that
the candidate worked.

*Origin:* G29 as a design concern, upgraded to a measured defect by G48.
*Evidence, in the tree rather than in a report:*
`packages/react-native-matrix-crypto/interop/suite.ts:174` records that a release build on
an emulator reported `signal FAIL (none)` on five of eight launches and passed on the other
three, with nothing else different, while the same code in a debug build passed every time.
`suite.ts:56` records that a 2000 ms budget still failed one launch in eight and 15000 ms
passed eight of eight, and `SIGNAL_WAIT_MS = 15000` is the constant that resulted. The
callback is never lost; it can simply take seconds.
*The suspect, named with its reasoning so nobody re-derives it:*
`rust/matrix-crypto-core/src/observer.rs:56` is

```rust
pub(crate) fn emit(observer: &Arc<dyn ProbeObserver>, signal: ProbeSignal) {
    let observer = Arc::clone(observer);
    std::thread::spawn(move || observer.on_signal(signal));
}
```

one operating system thread per signal. Correct and cheap while one probe signal exists and
fires once. M3 routes real crypto events through this path.
*The candidate, and why the obvious alternative is wrong:* `tokio::spawn` onto the runtime
the core already owns is probably actively wrong. A foreign callback blocks synchronously
for as long as JavaScript likes, and the runtime has two worker threads shared with the
crate's async work, so two blocking listeners would stall the runtime that also drives
encryption. `std::thread::spawn` has no such failure mode, which is very likely why it was
chosen. The shape that gets both is **`spawn_blocking` on the same runtime**: a managed,
reusable pool rather than an unbounded thread per event. Two independent analyses reached
that conclusion, the implementer's and the Task 8 reviewer's.
*What was deliberately not done:* the race was patched at the observer with a bounded wait,
not at the source, because reopening `observer.rs` at the end of M2 would have disturbed
code three reviews had just validated. The user confirmed the deferral.
*Cost if undone:* a product-visible latency on signals that are supposed to be silent and
cheap, plus an unbounded thread spawn rate once verification and key events flow through
the channel.

*Done, and what the measurement said (measured 2026-08-28, re-measured with identified
arms 2026-08-29).* `emit` hands its work to `runtime::spawn_blocking_detached` rather than
spawning a thread. The mechanism half of this item is closed, and closed by a test rather
than by reading the file: `observer.rs`'s `a_burst_is_delivered_without_a_thread_per_signal`
emits 2000 signals concurrently and counts the threads that deliver them -- 512 at most,
tokio's blocking pool cap, against exactly 2000 for a thread per signal -- so the exit
criterion's "no longer spawns one operating system thread per signal" fails a test if it
ever stops being true.

**The latency half did not go the way this item predicted, and the finding is that rather
than a confirmation.** Two things written above this note are now known to be wrong. They
are left standing, per this document's convention, and named here instead: this item's
heading, *"Signal delivery latency, measured in seconds in a release build"*, and the
evidence line *"The callback is never lost; it can simply take seconds"*. Neither survives
the before-and-after pair. The seconds were never measured -- what was measured was a
pass/fail check at a 2000 ms budget -- and the mechanism they were attributed to has now
been run side by side with its replacement without producing them.

*The measurement.* Full procedure, arm identification and every sample in
`docs/measurements/2026-08-29-signal-delivery-latency.md`; the harness is
`scripts/measure-signal-latency.sh`. On the arrangement §7 Q8 requires -- release build,
emulator-5554, API 35, before any verification traffic on the channel -- 40 launches,
20 on each side of the change, interleaved launch by launch, 28 of them with the host
deliberately saturated (10 by CPU, 18 by disk):

| arm | host load | n | min | median | p90 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `before` | idle | 6 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| `before` | CPU-saturated | 5 | 2 | 5 | 22.6 | 33 |
| `before` | disk-saturated | 9 | 1 | 2 | 25 | 37 |
| **`before`** | **all** | **20** | **0** | **2** | **23.1** | **37** |
| `after` | idle | 6 | 1 | 4.5 | 14 | 16 |
| `after` | CPU-saturated | 5 | 7 | 20 | 45.6 | 56 |
| `after` | disk-saturated | 9 | 1 | 5 | 29.4 | 59 |
| **`after`** | **all** | **20** | **1** | **9.5** | **32.6** | **59** |

All 40 passed `PROBE_SUMMARY 12/12`. The seconds did not reproduce on either arm, so the
emission mechanism was not what the fifteen seconds were paying for. What did reproduce is
the race: the callback lands after the promise in 20 of the 40 launches, at millisecond
scale.

*Which way the difference runs, because the arms are not indistinguishable.* The blocking
pool is the slower of the two: median 9.5 ms against 2 ms, higher in every host condition
separately, and widest under CPU saturation at 20 ms against 5 ms. The tails overlap -- p90
32.6 ms against 23.1 ms, worst 59 ms against 37 ms -- so the separation is in the body rather
than the tail.

*How strongly, said out loud, because the previous round said nothing.* One star. Of the 20
interleaved pairs, 12 favour the new path being slower, 6 run the other way and 2 tie; a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives p ~ 0.04, a paired sign-flip permutation on the median
p ~ 0.03, and the same permutation on the mean p ~ 0.08 -- the mean is dominated by two
outliers, a -35 ms and a +58 ms pair. Real, and not more than that, from n = 20 per arm at
millisecond clock granularity.

*And the cause is not established.* An earlier draft of this note attributed the gap
confidently to the first `emit` building the tokio runtime. That does happen on this path and
it is one-off per process -- `observer::emit` says why -- but it does not account for the
gap's shape, and this note said it did. Two things are now measured instead of argued:

* **The excess is confined to the first signal of a process.** Timing a second signal in the
  same process makes the two paths indistinguishable: a median gap of 0 ms, against 22 ms on
  the first signal under the same CPU saturation. So whatever it is, it is a first-use cost,
  and no product pays it per signal.
* **It is not fixed work.** The arms share a floor -- launches on both deliver in 0-1 ms --
  which bounds any constant added cost far below the observed median gap; and the gap grows
  with contention rather than staying put. That is the signature of exposure to scheduling,
  not of a constant amount of work.

Which first-use step dominates -- building the runtime, creating the first pool thread, or
simply having more handoffs to be descheduled between -- is not separated by anything measured
here, and is recorded as open rather than guessed at a third time.

*The two arms were provably different binaries, and the first attempt at this could not
say that.* Android imports the Rust library as a prebuilt from a gitignored `jniLibs/` with
no Gradle edge back to the crate, so an arm swap that skipped `ubrn build android` would
have produced two identical APKs -- and two indistinguishable distributions, which is what
was reported. `observer::EMIT_BUILD` now compiles a fingerprint of the emission path's
source into the artifact and `probe` appends it to `core_version`, so every launch says
which `emit` it is running; the harness refuses to record a run whose arms report the same
value. The `before` arm ran `8e8c3246`, the `after` arm `9c223b45`, on every one of the
40 launches.

*What else the latency is: §8 asks, and the honest answer is that no candidate has been
confirmed.* **The 1-in-8 failure at a 2000 ms budget is unexplained and stays open.** What
is now known about it:

* **It may never have been lateness.** The original observation used a pass/fail check with
  no instrument, so `signal FAIL (none)` at 2000 ms cannot distinguish a late callback from
  one that never arrived. The 8-of-8 pass at 15000 ms does not settle it either: at a
  1-in-8 loss rate, eight clean launches happen 34% of the time. So the M2 conclusion
  "the callback is never lost, it can simply take seconds" rested on evidence that could
  not tell those two apart.
* **A persistent loss at that rate is now refuted**, which is this measurement's one
  positive contribution to the mystery rather than a failure to reproduce it: 40 launches
  delivered 40 callbacks, and a persistent 1-in-8 loss survives that with probability
  0.5%.
* **Host saturation was tried as the candidate.** The original observation was taken while
  this repository was being built, which contends for CPU, disk and page cache at once.
  28 of these launches ran under deliberate saturation, 10 CPU-bound and 18 disk-bound.
  Neither reproduced anything resembling it: the worst delivery under either kind was 59 ms,
  against the 2000 ms the original observation defeated. Note what that does and does not
  exclude -- busy loops and file churn are not a Gradle and cargo build, which also contends
  for memory bandwidth and page cache.
* **What would settle it** is the expensive part, which is why it is deferred rather than
  answered: enough launches, under a genuine concurrent build rather than synthetic load,
  to observe the event with the instrument attached. At roughly two minutes per launch, and
  a rate no better characterised than "about one in eight", that is hours of emulator time
  for one observation.

Shipping with this open is a judgement, not an oversight: 40 consecutive launches cannot
reproduce it, and the cost of chasing it exceeds what it currently obstructs. What is not
deferred is the consequence -- `SIGNAL_WAIT_MS` is re-derived to 10000 rather than to 1.5x
the budget that was watched failing, because a budget sized against the distribution that
excludes the phenomenon is not a margin against the phenomenon. `ProbeHarness.tsx` logs
`PROBE_SIGNAL_MS`, `PROBE_PROMISE_MS` and `PROBE_EMIT_BUILD` on every probe run, so
sensitivity to a seconds-scale regression comes from the instrument rather than from the
budget, and the next person re-measures rather than inherits. The line numbers cited in the
evidence above have moved; the file and the constant names still resolve.

*One thing this note cannot fix from here, named rather than left.* The commit that made
this change, `971bcce`, is typed `perf(core)`, and the direction turned out to be the other
way: on the first signal of a process the new path is the slower one. Its body also presents
the sequential microbenchmark -- "two thousand signals emitted one at a time" -- as the
evidence for the change, and that is the regime `a960f83` retired as the wrong question when
it landed the concurrent burst test. Nothing in the tree consumes the commit type, and this
document's own convention is to append and name rather than rewrite, so the type stands and
this sentence is the correction. Retyping to `refactor(core)` before the branch merges is
the cleaner option and is still open.

*What the change is worth, given that it costs latency rather than saving it.*
Measured on the host,
which is a different question from the one above and is labelled as such.
`a_burst_is_delivered_without_a_thread_per_signal` emits 2000 signals with none of them
waited on -- the concurrent burst this item's cost line names ("once verification and key
events flow through the channel"), not the sequential one an earlier draft of this note
generalised from -- and delivers all of them across at most tokio's 512 pool threads, in
tens of milliseconds. A thread per signal delivers the same burst across exactly 2000
distinct operating system threads, which is what the test fails on. **That is the unbounded
thread spawn rate this item's cost line names, closed**, and the word is earned by a test
in the tree rather than by a microbenchmark in someone's notes. It is not free: the pool's
cap is tokio's 512 and is shared with `matrix-sdk-sqlite`'s connection pool, so a listener
population that parks hundreds of pool threads would delay the crypto store -- and, in the
direction this item is about, the store's own blocking work can now delay a signal, which a
thread per signal made impossible. `runtime.rs` carries the bound that makes the second
half acceptable rather than open.

#### B6. `gate:stubs` needs a four-line second opinion

*Not in M3.*

*Origin:* G51.
*Evidence and the ruling behind it:* two gates deliberately use two different oracles.
`scripts/generated-paths.txt` is a list and asks "is this file supposed to be generated",
which is intent; `gate:stubs` sniffs a file's own header and asks "does this still look
generated", which is observed fact. **The gap between intent and fact is the defect
signal**, so they were ruled not to be unified: moving `gate:stubs` onto the list would make

*What is left undone:* give `gate:stubs` a second opinion rather than a replacement oracle,

intent-versus-fact check from the 5 artifacts it inspects to the 19 files the list's 9 entries
resolve to.
*Cost if undone:* the compound case stays open, and it is the one that matters: hollowing a
shipped file fails the gate with its header intact and passes with the header stripped. The
edit that hollows the file is the same edit that disarms the check meant to catch it.

#### B7. The withheld-code retry gap: ruled, never dispatched

*Not in M3, and it has the sharpest product-visible edge of anything deferred here.* A
product that reads `retriable` and follows it retries a deliberate refusal forever, and a
consumer building against `0.1.0` can reach that today. What makes it deferrable rather
than urgent is that the ruling is already written and needs no design pass: it can land in
any later version as specified, by whoever next owns `session.rs`, the FFI mirror and
`errors.ts` at the same time.

*Origin:* G26. This is the one inherited item where a ruling exists and the work did not
happen, so it is stated with both.
*The ruling:* split the kind rather than add a field. `unshared_session` keeps the
circumstantial codes (`m.unavailable`, `m.no_olm`) and stays retriable; the policy codes
(`m.blacklisted`, `m.unauthorised`) get their own non-retriable kind. A withheld code is a
protocol constant carrying no identifier, so this leaks nothing and keeps every error
fieldless.
*Why it did not land:* it touches `session.rs`, the FFI mirror and `errors.ts`, and another
task owned `matrix-crypto-ffi/src/lib.rs` at the time. It was scheduled as a later fix round
and never dispatched.
*Evidence it is still open:* `packages/react-native-matrix-crypto/src/errors.ts:90` is still
`RETRIABLE = new Set(['missing_key', 'unshared_session'])`, and `UnsharedSession`'s own doc
comment in `session.rs` now
documents the ambiguity instead of resolving it, warning that a product must not assume
every occurrence is productive to retry.
*Cost if undone:* a product following the runtime retries a deliberate refusal forever. The
sender blacklisted this device; every retry costs battery and network and can never succeed.

## 6. Testing: what the existing ladder means once verification exists

M1 §8.0 fixed the ordering and M2 executed it. M3 inherits the ladder and its costs, not a
new test shape.

* **Level 1**, two parties in one process, no server. Deterministic, fast, catches the large
  majority of defects. It cannot answer whether the wire format is right, because it tests
  this implementation against itself.
* **Level 2**, a real homeserver and a third-party client, in both directions. Currently
  proven at the core and through the facade, with the ratchet as its floor (§1.2).
* **Level 3**, cross-implementation coverage against both Synapse and Continuwuity, plus the
  federation pairing of B18. Never yet run, and not M3's: §7 Q9 takes the federated variant
  out and B18 sits in §5.2.

What is settled and must be carried into whatever M3 tests, because each of these was paid
for once already:

* Assert on the decoded event type, not on a request's `kind`. A `kind`-only assertion
  passes on a withheld notice, which is a message saying the key could not be sent.
* Corrupt a freshly produced artifact, never a copy of a delivered one, or the control tests
  replay detection (G42).
* Teardown must survive a panic, and must take ownership of a remote resource the moment its
  identifier is in hand rather than a few lines later. A failing run once leaked a device and
  a room on the operator's homeserver, and a mutation proof placed one line further down
  could not see the window that allowed it (G46, G53).
* A summary's denominator is pinned by something other than the artifact reporting it
  (§3.2).
* A test that requires credentials is not reproduced by a reviewer. It is verified by
  construction and labelled as such.

**What shape a verification proof takes at each level is not settled** and is §7, Q7. One
part of it is settled by Q1 rather than by Q7: SAS produces a comparison meant for a human,
so whatever proves the flow has to stand in for one, and a stand-in that always agrees
proves less than it appears to. That is why §8's first exit criterion asks for a run in
which the two sides genuinely disagree.

## 7. Design questions: what the scope decision closed, and what it did not

These are M3's design decisions. Each is stated with the options that exist and what each
option costs, and each now carries a status:

* **Settled** means the scope decision answered it. The answer and the reasoning are
  given, and the options are kept rather than deleted, because a deferred option is a
  decision with a price.
* **Narrowed, still open** means the scope decision removed some options or some costs and
  left a real choice.
* **Out of M3** means the scope decision took the work out of this milestone without
  deciding the design. The design question below it is untouched and stays available.
* **Open** means the scope decision did not reach it at all.

**Nothing here is answered by inference from the surrounding text.** Where a status says
open, it is open; the temptation is to read the four M3 items in §0.1 as though they
implied answers to the questions around them, and in six weeks a reader has no way to tell
an inferred answer from a decided one.

### Q1. SAS, QR, or both

**Settled: SAS only.** No new dependency, no lockfile change, no Cargo feature, no size
delta, and no camera anywhere near this library. QR is deferred, not rejected.

*The evidence behind the split, which is why the answer was cheap.* Upstream provides both.
`matrix-sdk-crypto 0.18.0` exposes `Sas`, `SasState`, `EmojiShortAuthString` and
`AcceptSettings` unconditionally, and `QrVerification`, `QrVerificationState` and
`ScanError` behind an off-by-default `qrcode` feature (`qrcode = ["dep:matrix-sdk-qrcode"]`).
`matrix-sdk-qrcode` is not currently in `rust/Cargo.lock`.

The options as they were weighed, kept because the deferral has a price and the price
should be visible to whoever picks QR up:

* **SAS only.** No new dependency, no lockfile change, no size delta, no camera anywhere
  near this library, and the emoji or decimal comparison is a pure data flow the facade can
  carry as values. Against that, it puts the whole burden of the check on a human comparing
  a short string on two screens, and every step a user must perform is a step a user can
  skip or perform carelessly.
* **QR only.** Fewer steps for the user to get wrong, at three costs: it adds a Cargo
  feature and a dependency to a tree whose size budget has already tripped once and was
  rescued by a linking change rather than by slimming; it raises the question of who owns
  the camera and the scanning surface, which is arguably a product concern this library is
  supposed to stay out of; and the bridge would have to carry a scanned payload across the
  boundary, which is a marshalling shape it has never carried.
* **Both.** Matches what real clients ship and what a user expects, at the cost of two state
  machines to expose, two sets of failure modes to classify, and roughly twice the level 2
  proof surface.

**What the deferral costs, stated so it is not rediscovered as a surprise.** The human
burden that SAS-only leaves standing is the whole of the first option's objection to
itself, and it does not go away by being chosen. It also makes §7 Q7's third bullet
unavoidable rather than conditional: the flow's output is a comparison meant for a human,
so whatever proves the flow has to stand in for one.

The size gate remains a live constraint rather than a hypothetical one, and the fallback M1
pre-agreed for it, per-platform packages resolved through `optionalDependencies`, was
examined during M2 and found not to work as written. SAS-only means M3 does not test that
fallback, and whoever adds QR meets it unbuffered.

### Q2. What the public surface for verification looks like, given the frozen facade

**Narrowed, still open.** Choosing SAS removes one of the three original costs, the
marshalling of a scanned payload the bridge has never carried, and leaves everything else
exactly as it was. This is a real design decision and M3 has to make it.

Three signatures are already frozen and typed (§2.4):
`requestVerification(userId, deviceId): Promise<string>`,
`confirmVerification(verificationId, data: unknown): Promise<void>`, and
`getDeviceStatuses(userId): Promise<DeviceStatus[]>`.

A verification flow is a multi-step state machine with two participants, cancellation, and
events arriving out of band. Two functions and an opaque id may or may not be able to
express it.

* **Keep them as frozen and carry everything else on the signal channel.** No breaking
  change, and the frozen shape was designed precisely so a product could compile early. But
  `data: unknown` repeats the shape B3 exists to complain about: a required, structured
  input with no compile-time help, in a security-critical call. M3 fixes that shape in one
  call and would be preserving it in another, in the same milestone.
* **Keep them and add functions rather than change them**, as M2 did with
  `takeOutgoingRequests` and `markRequestSent`, which were additions to the frozen surface
  rather than changes to it. Additive, but grows the surface, and each new function is
  another thing a product can forget to call. M2's own precedent argues against optional
  calls whose absence fails silently (the `trackUsers` rejection).
* **Break them under the G1/G9/G25 test.** Legitimate only if the frozen shape cannot
  express something required, which is exactly the standard §4 states. The question is
  whether it can.

Whatever is chosen, `requestVerification(userId, deviceId)` presupposes device-to-device
verification with a known device id. Verification requests in Matrix are also commonly sent
to a user rather than a device, and self-verification of a new login is a distinct flow. Both
bear on whether the frozen shape is sufficient, and Q9's half-open remainder is the same
question seen from the test side.

### Q3. How `verification_state` reaches a consumer without breaking `EventEnvelope`

**Open, and this is the one most likely to be misread as settled.** The scope decision says
`verification_state` ships in M3. It says nothing about its shape, and the shape is the
whole design. All three options below are still available and still cost what they cost.

Upstream's `VerificationState` is `Verified` or `Unverified(VerificationLevel)`, and
`VerificationLevel` has five variants, one of which carries a further sub-enum (§3.1). The
facade's existing `TrustState` is a **closed** union of three strings.

* **Add a field to `EventEnvelope`.** The most direct: the authenticity of an event travels
  with the event, which is where a product will look for it. It changes a returned record
  every consumer already destructures, and it means `encryptEvent` and `decryptEvent` return
  a type where one field is meaningful in only one direction, which the existing `algorithm`
  and `sender` doc comments already have to explain at length.
* **Return it separately**, for example a second value or a companion call keyed by
  something the envelope carries. Leaves the frozen record alone, at the cost of letting a
  product decrypt an event and never ask, which is the failure mode this whole item exists
  to prevent.
* **Carry it only on the signal channel.** Fits the existing `trust_changed` variant and
  keeps per-event surface unchanged, but a signal is not attached to an event, and the
  question a product asks is about a specific event.

One constraint on all three options, from upstream and not from us: `verification_state`
is documented as the state of the sending device **at the time of decryption**, and
upstream tells callers who persist it to mark it dirty when a device change arrives down
the sync (`matrix-sdk-common-0.18.0/src/deserialized_responses.rs:345`). So a value
carried on an envelope is a snapshot that can go stale, and a value fetched on demand is
current but requires the product to ask. That is a property of the data, not a preference,
and it bears on every option above.

Orthogonally: **how much of upstream's detail survives.** Collapsing five reasons into
`TrustState`'s three loses the distinction between "the identity is unverified", "the device
is unsigned" and "the sender does not match", which are different product responses.
Widening `TrustState` breaks a closed union. Adding a separate open tag beside it means two
trust vocabularies in one surface. And §2.5's lesson applies directly here: a fold does not
only lose information, it disables the test that would have caught the fold.

**One thing the scope decision does bear on.** With cross-signing half open (Q6), the value
a SAS-only M3 can actually produce may be a narrow subset of upstream's five levels. A
surface designed for the subset and a surface designed for the full enum are different
surfaces, and choosing the first is a decision to be made deliberately rather than by
discovering later that only two values ever appear.

### Q4. Whether `CollectStrategy` moves off `AllDevices`, and what that does to the current tests

**Out of M3.** Moving the outbound collect strategy is not one of the four items, it
depends on published cross-signing identities that §2.3 says do not exist, and its cost
below is measured in existing tests that would have to be re-examined. So M3 ships with
`EncryptionSettings::default()` and `CollectStrategy::AllDevices` outbound, and
`TrustRequirement::Untrusted` inbound, exactly as M2 does.

**The design question is untouched and everything below still stands.** What is decided is
the milestone, not the answer.

**And one thing this makes wrong rather than merely deferred.** Both construction sites
carry `// M2: verification lands in M3; revisit this with it.` (`session.rs:69` and
`session.rs:814`). Verification lands in M3 and this is not revisited with it, so that
comment becomes false the moment M3 closes. §8 requires it to say something true instead.

Once cross-signing exists and identities are published, the identity-based strategy upstream
recommends becomes available. Moving to it is not a switch.

* **Move.** Aligns with MSC4153 and with what upstream calls recommended, and it is the
  outbound half of the property M3 exists to add.
* **Stay, deliberately and with the comment rewritten.** `AllDevices` shares with every
  unblacklisted device, which is what makes today's tests work at all.

What moving costs, concretely and measurably, because it is already known:

* The level 2 run against a real account fanned the share to all 27 devices on that account.
  An identity-based strategy shares with a strict subset, so any assertion phrased in terms
  of "the key reached the counterparty" has to be re-examined against a counterparty whose
  identity may not be published at all.
* Level 1's property that exactly one to-device request carries the key is a two-device
  property rather than an infrastructure one, disclosed rather than discovered. It would need
  restating.
* `matrix-nio` is the counterparty in both level 2 proofs. Whether it publishes a
  cross-signing identity the strategy would accept determines whether the proofs still run at
  all, and that is a question to answer before the change rather than after.
* The inbound mirror, `TrustRequirement::Untrusted`, is a separate decision with the same
  trigger. Tightening it makes `UnknownDevice`'s second reason reachable (B8) and would
  reject events from unverified devices, which is a product-visible behaviour change and
  arguably a product policy rather than a library one.

### Q5. Where the verification state machine lives, given "one machine per process, no handle"

**Narrowed, still open.** Choosing SAS removes `QrVerification` from the set of handle-shaped
upstream objects, leaving `VerificationRequest` and `Sas`. Two is not one, so the question
survives intact.

M1 froze `createCryptoMachine` as returning `Promise<void>`, which decided that the machine
is held by the library rather than handed to JavaScript, and M2 honoured that rather than
reopening it. Upstream's verification API is handle-shaped: `VerificationRequest`, `Sas` and
`QrVerification` are objects with state.

* **Keep the ownership model and address flows by opaque id**, which the frozen
  `requestVerification` returning `Promise<string>` already assumes. Consistent with the rest
  of the surface, at the cost of a registry inside the core with a lifetime policy, an
  eviction rule and a "this id is unknown" error, all of which the outbound request pump
  already had to grow and which took two fix rounds to get right.
* **Return handles for verification only.** Matches upstream, at the cost of two ownership
  models in one library and a JSI object lifetime question the bridge has never had to
  answer.

Note that the pump's registry is a worked precedent worth reading before choosing: it keys by
transaction id, evicts superseded kinds, keeps entries on a failed resolution so the same id
can be retried, and its `pending` map had to be proven not to grow without bound.

### Q6. What M3 owns of cross-signing, secret storage, and recovery

**Half settled, and the open half is the one that can force itself back into M3.**

*Settled:* **secret storage and recovery are out.** `exportSecrets` and `importSecrets` stay
frozen and unimplemented, and the passphrase-handling surface they would open stays closed.
They are not among the four items and nothing a product builds on `0.1.0` needs them to
exist rather than to reject.

*Open:* **whether M3 needs cross-signing bootstrap.** Not as scope appetite, which the scope
decision has already spent, but as a fact about upstream that nobody has established here.
The first option below states the risk in exactly the right terms and it is not rhetorical:
if a SAS verification with no cross-signing identity in play never yields
`VerificationState::Verified`, then M3's second exit criterion cannot be observed and the
milestone's central claim cannot be demonstrated. **Establish that against upstream before
designing on top of it.** §1.2's whole argument is about the difference between reading an
implementation and running it.

The original three options, with the third now out:

* **Verification only**, deferring cross-signing bootstrap and secret storage. Smallest
  milestone, but it risks shipping a verification that cannot survive a reinstall, and B1's
  `verification_state` would rarely reach `Verified` in practice.
* **Verification plus cross-signing bootstrap**, without server-side secret storage. Makes
  verification meaningful within a device's lifetime.
* **All three**, including secret export and import. Out, by the scope decision.

M2's own split argument applies and cuts both ways: it separated encryption from trust
establishment precisely so that a failure would be unambiguous between "the encryption is
wrong" and "the trust plumbing is wrong". The same reasoning can separate verification from
recovery, or can argue that verification without durable identity is the ambiguous half.

### Q7. What counts as proof for verification, at each level of the ladder

**Open, and narrowed in the direction that makes it harder.** SAS-only means the flow's
output is a comparison meant for a human, so the third bullet below is now unavoidable
rather than one option among three. That is the cost of Q1's answer, paid here.

Level 1 for encryption meant two machines in one process. The verification equivalent is
plausible but not obviously equivalent, since a verification flow is symmetric and
interactive, and the emoji or decimal comparison it produces is meant for a human.

* **Level 1 only**, two machines in one process completing a flow. Deterministic and cheap;
  proves the state machine, not the wire format, and §1.2 is the whole argument for why that
  distinction matters.
* **Level 2 with a third-party client.** This is the check that catches a consistent
  misreading, and it requires the counterparty to implement the same verification flow. That
  needs establishing rather than assuming, for whichever counterparty is chosen, before it is
  designed on top of. Narrowed usefully by Q1: the question is now specifically whether the
  counterparty implements SAS, which is a smaller and more answerable question than whether
  it implements verification.
* **A human-in-the-loop step.** If the flow's output is an emoji comparison, then something
  has to stand in for the human, and a stand-in that always agrees proves less than it
  appears to. The mutation that must exist is a run where the two sides genuinely disagree
  and the flow refuses. §8's first exit criterion requires exactly that mutation, so this
  bullet is a requirement rather than an option.

### Q8. Whether verification state changes ride the existing signal channel

**Open, with its stated precondition now met.** This question closed with the sentence
"B2 should be decided first or explicitly at the same time, because choosing the channel
without resolving the latency is choosing the latency." B2 is in M3, so the sequencing
obligation is discharged and the objection to the first option loses its force. The choice
itself is untouched, and it is still a choice.

`CryptoSignal` already declares `trust_changed` with a `TrustState`, and the channel is
specified as typed, silent by default, and taking no product decision.

* **Use it.** The variant exists and is unused, and trust changes are exactly the "no call in
  flight" case the channel was designed for. But B2 is unresolved, and this is the change that
  turns the channel from one probe signal firing once into a real event stream: the latency
  measured in seconds and the thread-per-signal design become product-visible at the same
  moment.
* **Add a call-shaped surface instead**, and let the product poll or await. Avoids B2
  entirely, at the cost of a product that has to ask rather than be told, which is what a
  channel exists to prevent.

Note the ordering B2 creates rather than removes: if verification state rides the channel
**and** B2's fix lands in the same milestone, then the first real consumer of the channel
and the first change to its delivery mechanism arrive together, and a latency measured
after both cannot attribute itself. Measure B2's before-and-after on the arrangement that
exists today, which is what §8 asks for, rather than on the one verification introduces.

### Q9. Whether M3's verification proof needs federation at all

**Half settled.** *Settled:* the federated variant is out. B18's federation pairing is in
§5.2 and M3 does not run it, which is consistent with what M2 already found: Matrix
end-to-end encryption is device-to-device, and federation was never the thing under test.
*Open:* which of the two non-federated scopes M3's proof uses, which is a real choice and
interacts with Q2 and Q7.

M2 assumed level 2 needed two accounts and stalled on provisioning one, until it turned out
that Matrix end-to-end encryption is device-to-device, so **one account with two devices
exercises the same cryptographic path** (G37). The mistake recorded there was forwarding a
requirement without asking why it existed.

The same question is open, and genuinely open, for verification: self-verification of a
second device on one account and verification between two users on one homeserver are two
different scopes with two different infrastructure costs, and they exercise different
upstream flows. Verification across federated homeservers is the third, and it is the one
the scope decision removes. B18 records what the user has already decided about the
federation pairing when it happens; it does not decide that M3's verification proof needs
it, and M3's answer is that it does not.

## 8. Exit criteria

Falsifiable statements, in the shape M2 §10 used: each names what would have to be
observed, not what would have to be believed. These are written for the four-item M3 of
§0.1. The larger milestone's criteria have been removed rather than softened, because a
criterion nobody intends to meet teaches a reader to skip the list.

The ones that depend on a question §7 leaves open are worded so that they do not
presuppose its answer.

M3 closes when all of the following hold:

* **Two parties complete a SAS verification**, and the flow is proven to be able to fail: a
  run in which the two sides' short authentication strings genuinely disagree ends in a
  refusal, observed rather than asserted. A stand-in that always agrees proves less than it
  appears to (§7 Q7).

* **A decrypted event carries an authenticity claim to the consumer**, in whatever shape
  §7 Q3 settles, and the claim is observed changing for the same sending device across a
  verification, in one test, through the public TypeScript surface. The claim is stated as
  "changing" rather than "reaching `Verified`" deliberately: whether a SAS-only M3 can
  produce upstream's `Verified` at all is §7 Q6's open half, and this criterion must not
  presuppose the answer. Whatever the value turns out to be, it must be different before and
  after, and the difference must be the verification.

* **The claim is proven to be derived rather than echoed**: severing the wiring between
  upstream's `verification_state` and the public surface turns exactly the assertions about
  authenticity red, and leaves the decryption assertions green.

* **A third-party client participates in at least one SAS flow over a real homeserver**, in
  whichever direction and at whichever level §7 Q7 settles, or an explicit written finding
  that no available third-party counterparty implements SAS in a form this can drive, with
  the evidence for that finding. The second outcome is acceptable; silence is not.

* **`receiveSyncChanges` takes a typed parameter.** Observable form: a call site passing a
  raw `/sync` field name, or one of the five names misspelled, fails `tsc` rather than at
  runtime, proven by type-test cases that are watched failing. And the existing runtime
  guard survives: the test that a non-empty object naming no recognised field is rejected
  is still green. B3's own note is the reason both halves are required, and a type that
  replaces the guard rather than supplementing it loses ground the guard already holds.

* **`observer.rs` no longer spawns one operating system thread per signal**, and the
  release-build measurement that produced `SIGNAL_WAIT_MS = 15000` is re-run on the same
  arrangement and recorded as a before-and-after pair rather than an after alone. If the
  measurement does not move, the recorded finding names what else the latency is, which is
  a different outcome from not measuring. §7 Q8 adds one constraint on how: take the
  measurement on today's arrangement, before verification traffic is on the channel, or the
  two changes cannot be told apart.

* **No new Rust dependency entered the tree for verification.** Observable form:
  `grep -n "matrix-sdk-qrcode" rust/Cargo.lock` returns nothing, no `qrcode` feature is
  enabled, and the packed tarball is re-measured against the same corrected metric and
  compared to the gate rather than to the previous row alone. Q1's answer is what makes
  this checkable instead of a re-measurement of an unknown delta.

* **All existing gates stay green**, including `gate:drift` on regenerated bindings and
  `gate:agility` on every public identifier verification adds. None of them may name an
  algorithm. Confirm that against the gate rather than assuming it: the denylist is
  `megolm`, `olm` and `room`, so a SAS vocabulary is very likely clear, and "very likely"
  is what §3.2 is about.

* **`PROBE_SUMMARY` still passes on device with its denominator pinned host-side**, so M3
  is proven not to have broken M1 or M2.

* **Both READMEs describe what shipped, in both copies.** Observable form: the status
  table's device-verification row says SAS and says QR is not attempted rather than
  implying both arrive together; the sender-authenticity row no longer reads "not
  provided"; the roadmap does not promise as M3's what M3 did not do; and `gate:readme`
  proves the two copies identical. No capability appears as proven in one section and
  outstanding in another.

* **`session.rs` no longer promises a revisit that did not happen.** Observable form:
  `grep -rn "verification lands in M3" rust/` returns nothing, or returns comments that
  say what was decided. Verification lands in M3 and §7 Q4 leaves both trust settings
  where M2 put them, so the comment at `session.rs:69` and `session.rs:814` becomes false
  on the day M3 closes unless it is rewritten. §2.2's own point is that the inherited
  default is the one nobody argues about.

* **§5.2 is still a true list.** No item is deleted, every item that closed during M3 is
  marked closed with the check that proves it, and any item whose evidence went stale is
  corrected rather than left to decay. This replaces M2's rule that every parked item needs
  a named owner, which §5.2 overrides deliberately and once. The replacement is weaker on
  purpose and is not weaker in the way that matters: an unowned item with live evidence can
  be picked up by anyone, and an owned item with dead evidence cannot be picked up at all.

## 10. Out of scope for M3, in the older and larger sense

This section is not §5.2 and should not be read as its continuation. §5.2 holds work this
project has decided it wants and has not scheduled. This section holds the product
boundaries M1 drew, which no milestone moves and which are restated because they are the
boundaries most likely to erode when a milestone starts touching trust: full network sync,
timeline, pagination, search, non-crypto application storage, conversation UI, audio and
video, push notifications, product-level group logic, contact discovery, permission policy,
and application-level inter-instance federation.

The bridge still performs no network request. Verification is a protocol conducted over
to-device messages, so it produces outbound requests exactly as encryption does, and it
travels through the same pump, under the same ordering obligation, with the product owning
transport. Nothing about M3 moves that boundary.

The SAS comparison itself sits on the same line. The emoji or decimal string is data this
library produces and hands over; presenting it, and collecting the human's answer, is the
product's. §7 Q1 deferred QR partly on the grounds that owning a camera and a scanning
surface would have crossed this line, and that reasoning only holds if SAS stays on the
correct side of it.
